## Notes:
“Bad bits” is really **bad characters** (usually called _badchars_) — and there’s a subtle but important point: the encoder doesn’t actually “fix” the badchars in the way the term might suggest. Let me break it down.

### What are badchars?

A badchar is any byte value that you **cannot** put in your payload because the target application will corrupt, truncate, or choke on it when it processes your input.

The classic example is the null byte `\x00`. In C, strings end at a null byte. So if your shellcode contains `\x00` and the app copies your input with something like `strcpy`, the copy stops dead at that byte — the rest of your payload never makes it into the buffer. Your exploit dies.

Other common offenders and _why_ they’re bad:

- `\x0a` (`\n`, line feed) and `\x0d` (`\r`, carriage return) — anything reading input line-by-line stops here.
- `\x20` (space) — often a delimiter in parsers.
- `\x2f` (`/`), `\x5c` (`\`) — path separators, mangled by things handling filenames/URLs.

Look at your `-b` list and you’ll see exactly those: `\x00`, `\x0a`, `\x0d`, `\x20`, `/`, `\`, `=`, `;`, etc. Someone determined, for _this specific target_, that those byte values get destroyed in transit.

### How you find them (the part worth understanding)

Badchars aren’t universal — they depend on the app. You find them by sending all 256 possible byte values (`\x00` through `\xff`) into the buffer, then attaching a debugger and comparing what’s **in memory** against what you **sent**. Any byte that’s missing, changed, or that caused the input to get cut short is a badchar. You add it to the list and repeat until the block of bytes in memory matches your send perfectly. That curated list is what you hand to `-b`.

### What the encoder actually does to them

Here’s the key mental model: **the encoder doesn’t modify or “clean” the badchars.** Your raw shellcode happens to contain some of these forbidden byte values scattered through it. The encoder’s job is to **transform the entire payload into a new sequence of bytes that is functionally identical but contains none of the forbidden values.**

Roughly:

1. It takes your raw shellcode and scrambles it — for `alpha_mixed`, it re-expresses every byte using only alphanumeric values; for the default `shikata_ga_nai`, it XORs it against a key.
2. The scrambled version is now just data — inert, and (if encoding succeeded) free of every byte in your `-b` list.
3. It prepends a small **decoder stub** — a few instructions that, at runtime, unscramble the payload back into the original working shellcode _in memory_ and then jump to it.

So the flow at exploitation time is: the clean encoded bytes travel safely into the buffer → EIP lands on the decoder stub → the stub rebuilds your real shellcode in memory → your real shellcode runs.

The badchars never existed in what you sent over the wire. They only reappear _after_ decoding, once the payload is already safely sitting in memory where the app’s string-processing can’t touch it anymore.
